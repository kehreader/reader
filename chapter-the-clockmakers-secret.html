Chapter One

Margaret knew something was wrong the moment she stepped into the clock shop.

It wasn’t the silence that unsettled her—she had expected that. The shop had been closed for six days, ever since her uncle’s death. What disturbed her was the absence of stillness within that silence.

Because one clock was still alive.

Tick.
Tick.
Tick.

The sound cut through the air like a heartbeat that didn’t belong in the room.

She stood just inside the doorway and let it close behind her. The bell above it should have rung, but it didn’t. It hung motionless, as though even sound had been abandoned there.

Outside, London dissolved into fog. The gas lamps along the street blurred into pale halos, and the world beyond the glass looked distant and unreal. Inside, however, everything felt too real—too heavy, too preserved.

Her uncle’s shop had always been full of noise when she was younger. He used to say clocks were never truly silent, even when broken.

“They remember time,” he had once told her, smiling without looking up from his workbench.

Now they had all stopped.

Every clock in the shop was frozen at the same hour.

The hour of his death.

Margaret swallowed hard and stepped farther inside. The floorboards creaked beneath her weight, and she instinctively slowed her breathing, as though the building might react to her presence.

She moved between the shelves, her fingers brushing against the glass cases. Dust clung to her skin. The air smelled of aged wood, machine oil, and something faintly metallic—like old coins left in rainwater.

Then she heard it again.

Tick.
Tick.
Tick.

It came from the back of the shop.

She followed it.

The clock sat alone on a narrow table, slightly tilted, as if it had been placed there in haste. It was unremarkable compared with the others—no gold trim, no intricate carvings, no visible maker’s mark.

And yet it was the only thing in the room that still believed in time.

Margaret stopped in front of it.

For a long moment, she did nothing. She simply listened.

The ticking was steady. Patient. Almost deliberate.

She reached out slowly.

Her fingertips touched the glass.

Warm.

She pulled her hand back at once, her breath catching. Glass was not supposed to be warm—not in a room that had been empty for nearly a week.

Her uncle had been found in the back room of the shop. That was what the police had told her: a simple explanation. There were no signs of forced entry and no witnesses. Just a man who had worked too long among machines that measured time.

And now this.

Margaret turned the clock carefully in her hands. Beneath its wooden frame, she found a small brass latch hidden almost seamlessly against the grain of the casing.

It didn’t belong there.

She hesitated, then pressed it.

A soft click sounded.

The back panel released.

Inside, there was no mechanism at all.

No gears. No springs. No movement.

Only a hollow space.

And within it, a folded piece of paper.

Her name was written on the front.

Margaret.

Her breath slowed.

The handwriting was unmistakable.

Her uncle’s.

But that was impossible.

He had died without leaving anything behind. At least, that was what she had been told—what everyone had insisted.

Her fingers trembled as she unfolded the letter.

The paper crackled softly in the stillness.

Only one sentence was written inside:

If you are reading this, they have finally found me.

A cold sensation spread through her chest.

Before she could react, she heard it.

The shop door opening behind her.

Slowly.

Deliberately.

She turned.

The bell above the entrance remained still.

No sound had been made.

And yet someone stood in the darkness between the shelves.
