Chapter One

Detective James Mercer stopped just inside the briefing room and let the door close behind him.

The sound was soft, but in the silence that followed, it felt unusually final—like something had been sealed shut.

The room was narrow and functional, designed for efficiency rather than comfort. A long metal table dominated the center, its surface scratched from years of case files being dragged across it. Above, a single fluorescent strip light hummed with a tired, uneven rhythm, casting a pale, clinical glow over everything it touched.

Six photographs lay beneath the light.

Six images.

Six separate crime scenes spread across the table like pieces of a puzzle that refused to connect.

James didn’t speak immediately. He studied them first, letting his eyes move from one to the next.

A burned-out apartment kitchen.

A rain-soaked street outside a closed pharmacy.

A wooded area with police tape cutting through the trees like a warning.

A motel room with a shattered mirror.

A parking-garage stairwell.

And finally, a riverbank at dusk, the water dark and still.

Six locations that, on their own, meant nothing together.

Until someone had placed them side by side.

James reached out and picked up the third photograph.

The paper was slightly warm from the light above, as if it had absorbed the heat of the room.

“Who found these?”

His voice was low and controlled, but it carried enough weight to make the officer beside him straighten instinctively.

“Officer Mills.”

James didn’t look up.

“Where is Mills?”

There was a brief hesitation.

“Outside. Taking a call.”

James nodded once, as if filing the information away without interest, though his attention never left the photograph.

The image showed a kitchen.

A small one. Cramped. Poorly lit.

A chair had been knocked over, its legs twisted awkwardly against the tile floor. Broken glass glittered near the sink, catching what little light there was. A body lay partially visible near the edge of the frame—just enough to confirm what the scene was, but not enough to dominate it.

Standard-procedure photography.

Nothing unusual.

Nothing that should have drawn attention.

James shifted the photograph slightly, angling it toward the light.

That was when he saw it.

The window.

At first glance, it was just another background detail—dark glass reflecting the interior of the room. But reflections rarely stayed simple when you looked at them long enough.

Someone was there.

Not inside the kitchen.

Outside.

Standing across the street.

James leaned closer, narrowing his focus until the rest of the image seemed to fall away.

A man stood in the reflection.

Dark coat.

Collar turned up.

Hat pulled low over his brow.

He wasn’t looking at the scene.

He was looking at the camera.

James set the photograph down slowly, then reached for the fourth without breaking eye contact with the table.

The second image showed a different location.

A narrow street this time, lined with parked cars and flickering streetlights. Rain had left the pavement slick, reflecting neon signs in fractured colors.

And there he was again.

The same man.

Different street.

Different night.

Same coat.

Same posture.

Same deliberate stillness.

James felt something shift in the room—not physically, but perceptibly, like the air itself had become aware of what he was seeing.

He picked up the fifth photograph.

Again.

The man.

The parking-garage stairwell.

Half-hidden behind a concrete pillar.

Watching.

The sixth photograph followed.

The riverbank.

Dusk fading.

The man standing farther away this time, but still unmistakable.

Still present.

Still watching.

James placed all six photographs together in a single line.

His movements were precise now, controlled, almost mechanical.

“Get Mills.”

The officer beside him frowned, confusion tightening his expression.

“Why? What are you seeing?”

James didn’t answer immediately.

Instead, he tapped the first photograph—the kitchen scene—and then the reflection in the window.

“Because whoever took these pictures wasn’t documenting the murders.”

He paused.

The room felt smaller now, as if the walls had shifted inward.

“They were documenting the person taking them.”
