The Crown of Ash

AuthorAdrian Blackwood
GenreDark Fantasy
Length112,000 words
View full before & after (PDF)

Original Manuscript

Before editing — opening lines

The king was dead. Everyone knew it. His son, Rowan, was standing in the throne room looking at the crown. He knew he had to become king. The people outside were angry. There were soldiers everywhere. His adviser told him that he should put the crown on. Rowan didn't want to.

He remembered what his father had told him. "The crown belongs to whoever is willing to pay for it." Rowan didn't understand what he meant. Now he did. There was blood on the throne.

Editing Issues Identified

  • The opening lacked a distinctive voice.
  • The political conflict needed clarification.
  • Rowan's emotional response was underdeveloped.
  • World-building was introduced too generally.
  • The crown needed symbolic significance.
  • The chapter needed stronger tension.

Editing Work

I gave the opening a distinctive voice, clarified the political stakes, deepened Rowan's emotional response, and built the crown into a recurring symbol — while keeping the author's core story and structure intact.

Edited Manuscript

After editing — the same opening, revised

The king died before dawn. By sunrise, three armies were already marching toward the capital. Rowan stood alone beneath the black banners of House Veyr, staring at the crown his father had worn for thirty-one years. It was smaller than he remembered. As a child, he had imagined it as something enormous — golden, magnificent, impossible to lift. Now it looked like what it truly was: a circle of blackened metal. A prison disguised as a symbol.

"Your Highness." Lord Marrec stood behind him. "Put it on." Rowan didn't move. Beyond the doors, thousands of voices filled the square — some shouting his name, others shouting for his death. He crouched by the throne. A thin red line disappeared beneath the stone. Not his father's blood. Someone else's.

His father had once told him the crown belonged to whoever was willing to pay for it. Rowan had thought it was a metaphor. Now he wasn't so sure. For the first time, he understood what his father had meant: the crown did not belong to the king. The king belonged to the crown.
